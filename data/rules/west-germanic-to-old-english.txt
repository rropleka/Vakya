# West Germanic → Old English (Early West Saxon, the dialect of standard Old English spelling)
#
# Sources (retrieved 2026-11-07):
#   Wikipedia, "Phonological history of Old English", section "Phonological processes", which lists
#     the changes "chronologically in roughly the order described"
#     https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phonological_history_of_Old_English
#   Wikipedia, "Germanic umlaut", section "I-mutation in Old English" (the West Saxon column)
#     https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germanic_umlaut
#
# Changes the page gives only for other dialects (second fronting, Anglian smoothing, Anglian
# retraction) are left out. NOTE: lines are decisions the page doesn't settle.

from: West Germanic
to: Old English

class C = [type=Consonant]
class V = [type=Vowel]
class N = {m, n}
class R = {r, l}
# front and back vowels
class E = {[type=Vowel backness=Front], [type=Vowel backness=Near-front]}
class B = [type=Vowel backness=Back]
# voiceless fricatives, for nasal absorption
class F = {ɸ, f, θ, s, x}
# palatal consonants, for palatal diphthongisation
class P = {tʃ, ʝ, j, ʃ}

Absorption of nasals before fricatives: a nasal is lost before a voiceless fricative, lengthening the
vowel; a becomes a long nasal vowel (later ō).
# NOTE: the page only names the change and refers to the "Ingvaeonic nasal spirant law" article; the
# rule shape (ɑ to nasal ɑ̃ː, other vowels lengthened) follows its examples five, mouth, us and the
# page's later remark that nasalised ą̄ was raised to ǭ.
    ɑN → ɑ̃ː / _F
    VN → V[+long] / _F

First a-fronting: ā is fronted to ǣ unless followed by n, m or nasalised.
    ɑː → æː // _N

Raising before nasals: nasalised ą̄ and the sequences ān, ām, which first a-fronting left alone, are
raised to ǭ, ōn, ōm.
# NOTE: the page says only "later raised". It's placed before monophthongisation because the new ā
# from ai doesn't raise (stān "stone", not *stōn).
    ɑ̃ː → õː
    ɑː → oː / _N

Monophthongisation: ai becomes ā (after first a-fronting, so it stays ā).
    ɑi → ɑː

Second a-fronting (Anglo-Frisian brightening): a is fronted to æ unless followed by n or m or
nasalised; n and m block it only in stressed syllables.
    ɑ[+stressed] → æ // _N
    ɑ[-stressed] → æ

Diphthong height harmonisation: æu becomes ēa, eu becomes ēo, iu becomes īu.
    æu eu iu → æːɑ eːo iːu

Breaking: short front vowels become diphthongs before h, w, or r/l plus a consonant; long ī and ǣ
before h only.
# NOTE: the page says breaking doesn't happen before ll from West Germanic gemination of l + j; rj and
# lj are excluded on the same grounds (*harjaz > here, not *hiere).
    æ → æɑ / _x, _rC, _lC // _rj, _lj, _llj
    e → eo / _x, _rC, _lx, _lk, _w // _rj
    i → iu / _x, _rC, _w // _wi
    iː æː → iːu æːɑ / _x

A-restoration: æ is backed to a when a back vowel is in the next syllable.
    æ → ɑ / _C(C)B

Loss of final short vowels: final non-high short vowels are lost in words of more than one syllable.
# NOTE: the West Germanic page puts the loss of final -a there; it's here because the Old English
# page's own example (*faraną > *faraną > *faran, step 5 after a-restoration) needs it after
# a-restoration.
    {ɑ, æ, ɑ̃, æ̃}[-stressed] → ∅ / V(C)(C)(C)_#

Loss of nasality: vowel nasality, which lasted at least until a-restoration, is lost.
    V[+nasal] → V[-nasal]

Final j: j left at the end of a word after a consonant becomes i (see the West Germanic file).
    j → i / C_#

Palatalisation: k, g, sk and ɣ become palatal in front-vowel environments.
# g was [ɡ] after n or doubled and [ɣ] elsewhere; that split is made first.
# NOTE: sk is handled before k so that sk becomes ʃ rather than s plus ċ.
    ɣ → g / n_
    ɣɣ → gg
    sk → ʃ / #_
    sk → ʃ / _{i, iː, j}
    sk → ʃ / E_ // _B
    k → tʃ / _{i, iː, j}
    k → tʃ / #_E
    k → tʃ / i_, iː_, in_, iːn_ // _B
    gg → dʒdʒ / _{i, iː, j}
    g → dʒ / _{i, iː, j}
    ɣ → ʝ / _E, _j
    ɣ → ʝ / E_ // _B
# "by now [ɡ] was the form used in initial position"
    ɣ → g / #_

Palatal diphthongisation (West Saxon): e, ē, æ, ǣ become ie, īe, ea, ēa after ċ, ġ and sċ.
# NOTE: stressed vowels only, and not the æ that already begins a diphthong (ċeald, not *ċeaald).
    e[+stressed] eː[+stressed] æ[+stressed] æː[+stressed] → ie iːe æɑ æːɑ / P_ // _V

I-mutation (West Saxon): vowels are fronted or raised when i, ī or j follows in the next syllable.
# NOTE: "next syllable" is approximated as "after up to three consonants". Every mapping is in one
# rule so they apply together (otherwise æ > e would feed e > i).
    æɑ æːɑ io iːu ɑ ɑː æ e o oː u uː → ie iːe ie iːe æ æː e i ø øː y yː / _C(C)(C){i, iː, j}
# "a > e particularly before nasal consonants": a before a nasal went to e, not æ.
    æ → e / _N(C)(C){i, iː, j}

Medial syncopation: short a, æ, e are lost in medial open syllables.
    {ɑ, æ, e}[-stressed] → ∅ / VC_CV

Loss of medial j: internal j is lost after a long syllable.
# The page: "It is possible that loss of medial -j- occurred slightly earlier than loss of -ij-, and in
# particular before high-vowel loss", needed for *nuttju > nytt (and *brugjō > bryċġ).
    j → ∅ / V[+long]C_V, V[+long]VC_V, VCC_V

High vowel loss: unstressed i and u are lost in an open syllable after a long syllable (a long vowel
or diphthong, or a vowel before two consonants); two short syllables count as one long one. Final
loss comes before medial loss.
# NOTE: "long vowel or diphthong" is read as long vowel or *long* diphthong (whose first element is
# long here); short diphthongs count as short, as the page's ġiefu (< *gebō) shows.
# A long diphthong (īe) has its length on the first half, so V[+long]VC covers it.
    {i, u}[-stressed] → ∅ / V[+long]C_#, V[+long]VC_#, VCC_#, V[-long]CV[-long]C_#
    {i, u}[-stressed] → ∅ / V[+long]C_CV, V[+long]VC_CV, VCC_CV

Loss of -ij-: internal ij is lost after a long syllable (just after high vowel loss), along with j
left behind when high vowel loss took the i of ij.
    {ij, j} → ∅ / V[+long]C_V, V[+long]VC_V, VCC_V

Back mutation (West Saxon): short e and i become eo and io before a single labial or liquid followed
by a back vowel.
# A vowel right after another vowel is the second half of a diphthong (ie), not a vowel of its own.
    e i → eo io / _{ɸ, f, β, w, l, r}B // V_

H-loss: h is lost between vowels and between r/l and a vowel, lengthening a preceding short vowel;
an unstressed vowel after it is absorbed into the long vowel.
# NOTE: written as three steps: lengthen the vowel (or a diphthong's first element), drop h with the
# absorbed vowel, drop h after r/l. The page's "vowel assimilation" of other vowels in hiatus isn't
# modelled beyond this.
    V → V[+long] / _(V)(R)xV // V_
    xV[-stressed] → ∅ / V_
    x → ∅ / R_V

Palatal umlaut: short e, eo, io become i before final ht, hs, hþ.
    {e, eo, io} → i / _x{t, s, θ}#

Unstressed vowel reduction: remaining long ō and ô become a; all other unstressed long vowels are
shortened; the short front vowels æ and i merge as e; u is written o before a consonant.
# NOTE: the page gives the endings -ung, -um and -uc as exceptions to u > o.
    {oː, ô}[-stressed] → ɑ
    V[+long -stressed] → V[-long]
    {æ, i}[-stressed] → e
    u[-stressed] → o / _C // _ŋ, _ng, _m, _k

Diphthong changes (Early West Saxon): io and īo merge with eo and ēo; front rounded ø, ø̄ unround to
e, ē.
# The ø unrounding is from the Germanic umlaut page ("ø̄ > ē": fø̄t > fēt).
    io iːo iu iːu → eo eːo eo eːo
    ø øː → e eː

Palatal reversion: ċ and ċġ revert to c and g before a consonant.
# Not between the two halves of a doubled palatal (ċċ, ċġ).
    tʃ dʒ → k g / _C // _tʃ, _dʒ

Labiodental f: the Proto-Germanic bilabial ɸ is the labiodental f of Old English.
# NOTE: not a change the sources discuss; both write the sound f. Made explicit so later stages get f.
    ɸ → f

Epenthesis: a vowel appears between a consonant and a final r.
# NOTE: not on the page as a rule; it mentions epenthesis only for sāwol. Here it's limited to final
# Cr (æcer).
    ∅ → e / C_r#
